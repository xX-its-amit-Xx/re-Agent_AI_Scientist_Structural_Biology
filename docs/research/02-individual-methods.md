# How individual thinkers organised information so that it produced insight

Evidence grades: **A** controlled or meta-analytic · **B** documented primary/archival
fact · **C** scholarly inference or self-report · **D** folklore.

Sources were fetched and read directly — Darwin Online, the Luhmann-Archiv, the 1706
Locke and 1923 Cajal full texts, Martin's prefaces to *Faraday's Diary*, Birch's Boyle,
Hooke's *Posthumous Works*, Richter's Leonardo, the Rutgers Edison Papers, the *Paris
Review* interviews — plus Crossref, PubMed and PMC for the empirical literature. Items
that remained paywalled are named as such rather than paraphrased from secondary
accounts.

## Claims that did not survive checking

**Darwin's "rule about writing down objections" does not exist as stated (D→B).** Every
occurrence of "rule" in the *Autobiography* was enumerated; none is that. Nor is there
any "one hour a day of dissent" — almost certainly a corruption of *"I have written for
nearly an hour on most afternoons"*, which is about drafting the autobiography. The
golden rule itself is verbatim-accurate and is a **capture discipline, not a review
ritual**: write the contrary fact down *the moment you meet it*, because the mind forgets
disconfirming evidence faster than confirming evidence.

**Luhmann's addresses are half-topical (B).** The *leading* number is a coarse topical
entry point — his own word is *Grobschematisierung*; only the branching tail is
arbitrary. And the fleeting / literature / permanent trichotomy that dominates the modern
literature is **Ahrens's, not Luhmann's**; the archival workflow is different, and better.

**Faraday's 16,041 numbered paragraphs do not span 1820–1862 (B).** Martin's prefaces
give an unbroken sequence from 25 August 1832 to 6 March 1860. The earlier quartos are
unnumbered and use vertical marginal headings "for ease of reference."

**"Twelve favourite problems" is Rota reporting Feynman's spoken advice (C)** — *Notices
of the AMS* 44(1), 1997, nine years posthumous, no Feynman-authored source — and it says
keep them *in mind*, not in a notebook.

**The "Feynman Technique" is not Feynman's, but his actual test is better (B).** From
"What Is Science?" (*The Physics Teacher* 7(6), 1969): *"Without using the new word which
you have just learned, try to rephrase what you have just learned in your own
language."* That is **jargon ablation**, not explain-to-a-child. It is a mechanical test,
and it is implementable.

**Edison's "10,000 ways that won't work" is apocryphal (D).** The root is Mallory's 1910
account: *"Results! Why, man, I have gotten a lot of results! I know several thousand
things that won't work."* Likewise Faraday's "Work. Finish. Publish." first appears five
years posthumously, hedged "It is on record that", with no record cited.

## Luhmann: the real mechanics (B)

Roughly 90,000 slips across two collections. DIN A6, **recto only**, so a slip could be
read without removing it. Paper cut by hand from used A4 and bank statements, to keep the
cabinet operable from the desk.

Every slip gets a **permanent position that is never changed again**, digits and letters
alternating, each level meaning "branches off the preceding slip", inserted *between*
existing slips. *Autopoiesis* — his flagship late concept — lives at `21/3d26g1i`.
Luhmann: *"there are no privileged places."* Schmidt's caveat matters: he sometimes
violated his own alternation rule, so "the number on its own does not really inform about
the relations between notes."

**Bibliography kept physically separate**, one title per slip. The reading workflow is
the part the popular literature gets wrong: keywords and page numbers went on the *verso
of the bibliography slip*, "extremely minimalist and not excerpts in the proper sense",
often one slip for a whole book, converted into filed slips in a second pass on another
evening. His reading criterion: *"I always read with an eye to the
Verzettelungsfähigkeit of books"* — the slippability. Slip 9/8d: *"one reads differently
when one attends to the possibility of slipping — not: excerpts!"*

**The register is deliberately sparse**: about 3,200 keyword entries for 67,000 slips,
one to four locations per keyword, completeness pursued in the *number of keywords* and
explicitly abandoned for locations. The rationale on slip 9/8b, traced by Schmidt to
Ashby's brain model: capacity comes not from point-by-point access but from *relations
between notes*.

What it made hard, all documented: nothing could be moved; errors were never removed
("revised by later entries but not eliminated"); linear reading was destroyed by
interleaving; no full-text search; **unlinked notes die** — *"a note that is not connected
to this network gets lost in the slip box, is forgotten by the slip box"*, which Schmidt
formalises as black holes and confirms empirically; and a permanent maintenance tax —
*"the slip box costs me more time than writing books."*

**Causation is not established (C).** The only first-order source is Luhmann's
self-report, and the Archive's own coordinator flags that such statements made the
Zettelkasten "a myth". Cevolini's deflation is worth keeping: *"in the use of a card index
as a surprise generator, there is nothing particularly surprising if one considers the
evolution of knowledge management in early modern Europe."*

**The most valuable single finding in this investigation concerns synthesis.** There is no
documented account of Luhmann laying slips out, and he denied that publications came from
copying the box. What *is* documented is that his **typescripts obeyed the same grammar as
his slips**: insertions typed on separate sheets and interleaved, sub-numbered in red,
"deletions of already-written passages happened extremely rarely", growth inward. He did
not switch representations in order to write. **He ran the same operation on prose.**

## Darwin (B)

The golden rule is justified by an asymmetry in forgetting. Where the memorandum went:
30–40 labelled portfolios; an index written at the back of each book he owned; a drawer of
abstracts for books he did not own; and the underrated part — **a freshly built general
classified index compiled at the start of each project** by reading across all the short
indexes. Project-scoped and disposable.

**Destruction as a filing operation.** He cut books in half, tore all but the relevant
page from pamphlets, and "letters were dissected like specimens". The same to his own
notebooks: 27 leaves cut from Notebook B, 43 from D, 39 from E, carrying brown-crayon
classification numbers, filed into subject portfolios — with a major episode dated
**December 1856, while writing the big species book**. Retrieval, not tidying.

**Synthesis was a dated, scheduled phase followed by a compression ladder.** Journal, 9
September 1854: "began sorting notes for Species Theory". Then 35 pages (1842), 230
(1844), a half-finished long version, and the *Origin* as "an abstract of an abstract" in
"thirteen months and ten days". He credits the mechanism directly: *"By this means I was
enabled to select the more striking facts and conclusions."* And he framed the phase as
falsifiable: *"How awfully flat I shall feel, if… the whole thing explodes like an empty
puff-ball."*

## Faraday: the best evidence that a failure record pays (B)

The retrieval chain is airtight and it is the single most useful documented episode for
our purposes.

10 September 1821 — records a failed attempt to detect any action of electricity on
polarised light. 1834 — tries again, fails, and **publishes the null circumstantially** at
*Experimental Researches* ¶¶951–955, where it gets an index entry. November 1845, opening
the paper announcing the Faraday effect (¶¶2147–8): *"These ineffectual exertions, and
many others which were never published, could not remove my strong persuasion… I recently
resumed the inquiry… and have at last succeeded in magnetizing and electrifying a ray of
light."* The footnote to "a former occasion" reads: *Experimental Researches, 951-955.*

**He retrieves a twenty-four-year-old failure by paragraph number and makes it the premise
of a discovery.** What made that possible is that the negative had a *permanent address*.

Also documented: paragraph numbers used as index keys in the published work; the numbering
used as a conceptual trace through a long investigation; explicit reasoning about
negatives (¶9033); failures published as *suspended* rather than closed (¶2717); and a
pencil line down pages already consumed in writing a paper.

**And the most instructive failure in the whole survey: he never finished the index to his
own diary.** Martin records — apparently in Mrs Faraday's hand — "Slips left loose and
unarranged". He indexed everything else obsessively. **The hand-built local
cross-reference web worked; the global subject index was never built, and its absence did
not stop him.**

His best-attested statement on note-keeping is the inscription in the 1822 *Chemical
Notes, Hints, Suggestions and Objects of Pursuit* — a queries book in which "whenever any
query found an answer, he drew his pen through it and added the date": *"I already owe much
to these notes, and think such a collection worth the making by every scientific man."*

## Boyle, Hooke and the Royal Society (B)

Oldenburg to Boyle, 24 November 1664: *"We must be very careful as well of regist'ring the
person and time of any new matter, as the matter itselfe, whereby the honor of the
invention will be reliably preserved to all posterity."* He described the journal as *"one
of these philosophical commonplace books"* — the periodical conceived as a **collective**
commonplace book.

**The Register Book is a priority device and the Society says so.** Papers were
transcribed "in order to establish their precedence for a particular discovery or idea".
Structurally: precedence came not from the private notebook but from **transcription into
an institutional register with a third-party date**. The notebook is evidence; the register
is title.

**Hooke's registering rule is the clearest statement anywhere of why a notebook beats a
paper:** *"at first let all Circumstances be entred as considerable… they can be easily
struck out, as soon as they are… prov'd insignificant, and if entred in few words they
will not take much room; besides there are many things, which though perhaps insignificant
to the present End or Design, yet [may] be very notable to some other."* His 1665 weather
register instantiates it: nine ruled titled columns, fifteen days a side so "the Scheme of
a whole Month may at one View be presented to the Eye", a **controlled vocabulary** because
the phenomena "want proper Names", and a final column for *"general Deductions… arising
from the comparing the several phaenomena together."*

Boyle separated narrative from interpretation typographically, confessed a cracked
receiver because "it becomes one, that professeth himself a faithful relator of
experiments not to conceal" it, and wrote an entire 1661 treatise classifying **why
experiments fail**.

Shapin's counterweight, read in full: *"There was not, nor can there be, any such thing as
a report which notes all circumstances"*, and reporting failure is exploitable — a critic
"could capitalize upon Boyle's reported failures, or… deconstruct Boyle's reported
successes by identifying further contingencies."

## Leonardo: superb page, failed archive (B)

The famous quotation is authentic (Codex Arundel f.1r) and it is **not** an apology for
untidiness — it is a diagnosed retrieval failure: *"this is to be a collection without
order… I shall have to repeat the same things several times… for the subjects are many and
memory cannot retain them [all]… And if I wished to avoid falling into this fault, it would
be necessary… that, not to repeat myself, I should read over all that had gone before."*
No index, no classification, no global cross-reference; Richter notes that Leonardo's
characteristic cross-reference points to something *on the same sheet*.

What genuinely worked: multi-view demonstration "exactly as if you had that same limb in
your hand and were turning it from side to side"; layered disassembly "so proceed by
degrees to avoid confusion"; and a deliberate schematic-plus-naturalistic pair. One
structural property saved the corpus — a topic is usually covered in words and pictures
together on **one sheet**, so it survived being cut up.

The consequence is documented: he "did not publish his findings and they had little to no
direct influence on subsequent science"; the friction manuscripts predate Amontons by 150
years with no effect. It took a state-funded digital project to build the retrieval layer
he promised himself in 1509.

## Edison: the organisation did not work, and the arithmetic proves it (B)

Founding resolution, October 1870: *"all new inventions I will here after keep a full
record"* — and Rutgers gives the motive in the same breath, *"in anticipation of patent and
contractual litigation."* Bound notebooks from 1878 "that would remain intact as a
permanent record" — tamper-evidence, a legal property. The notebook circulated as a
**work-assignment instrument**: Edison wrote the experiments to be tried and passed the
book to the staff member best adapted to them.

Rutgers on the filing: *"Edison did not, however, adopt a systematic method… related
materials within the books were often widely separated"*; after 1887 papers were "left on
shelves and floors". **The index is posthumous.**

The verdict is arithmetic. The Rutgers project was founded in 1978 *because* "the sheer
size and organizational complexity of the archive deterred researchers". Forty-eight years
on it holds about 153,600 documents of an estimated **five million pages** — order 3 %.
Long-range reuse travelled through human memory and machinery, not lookup.

## Cajal: the most relevant book to this problem (B)

*Advice for a Young Investigator*, chapter V, "Diseases of the will", names five types
including *bibliófilos y polígotas*, whose symptom list reads as a specification for a
badly designed research agent: *"encyclopedist tendencies; command of many languages, some
entirely useless; subscriptions only to obscure journals; hoarding every new book in the
shop window; assiduous reading of what matters but above all of what interests few; an
invincible laziness about writing; and estrangement from the seminar and the
laboratory."* Verdict: *"erudition has very little value when it does not represent the
preparation and prodrome of intense, persevering personal action."*

**On summaries, which belongs above any summarisation pipeline:** flee summaries and
manuals "as from the plague", because *"Quien resume, se resume a sí mismo"* — whoever
summarises, summarises himself; the abridger *"takes what pleases him or what he can digest
without effort: gives the principal as accessory, and vice versa."*

**He asks our exact question**: *"Before beginning a laboratory investigation, should one
exhaust the bibliography or not?"* — noting the risk of suggestibility and of a false
"impression of exhaustion of the subject". His two-branch answer: prudently, exhaust it
first, to avoid rediscovering known things and thereby neglecting *"las verdaderas lagunas
del tema"*, the real gaps; but where that is impossible, proceed anyway, because "not
having been influenced by them, we will have travelled different routes", and *"it is worth
a thousand times more to risk repeating discoveries than to renounce all attempt at
experimental inquiry."*

On writing up he adds a gate: *"if our thought still fluctuates between diverse conclusions
and we lack the full consciousness of having hit the mark, that is a sign of having
abandoned the laboratory too early."*

## Locke's index: the exact opposite design to Luhmann's (B)

From the 1706 text. Two pages ruled into 25 lines; **twenty letters** (K, Y, W dropped "as
useless"; Q folded into Z) × five vowels = **one hundred classes**, in a two-page index for
a whole volume. The head must "begin with some Considerable Word that is Essential to the
Matter treated of", and "upon these Two Letters depends the whole Use of our Index".
Overflow uses reciprocal page numbers: *"By this Reciprocal Reference of Numbers, one turns
over as one Leaf all those which are between them, as if they were join'd together."*

The underrated part is **provenance**: before writing anything he enters author, title,
volume, place and date of edition, and — "what ought never to be omitted" — **the total
number of pages in the whole book**, which thereafter fingerprints the exact edition, with
the page collected from written over that total. A compact edition hash plus a fractional
address, in 1686.

Bush's 1945 diagnosis is exactly of this system: *"It can be in only one place, unless
duplicates are used; one has to have rules as to which path will locate it… Having found
one item, moreover, one has to emerge from the system and re-enter on a new path."*

**The contrast is the design decision.** Locke makes filing cheap and lookup deterministic
by fixing the topic at capture. Luhmann refuses to fix it, pays a large filing cost, and
buys the possibility of arriving somewhere he did not intend.

## Nabokov, Benjamin, and the atomic-note story (B for facts, D for the moral)

Nabokov, *Paris Review* 40 (1967): *"**The pattern of the thing precedes the thing.** I
fill in the gaps of the crossword at any spot I happen to choose. These bits I write on
index cards until the novel is done."* Two things are usually missed: the pattern came
*first* — the cards filled a structure he already held — and the pencils had erasers.

**The instructive counter-case is Benjamin's *Arcades Project*:** convolutes keyed by
letter with bracketed section numbers, his text and his quotations typographically
distinguished, explicit boxed cross-references — **more addressing apparatus than Nabokov,
thirteen years' work, published only in 1982, unfinished.** Benjamin had the better filing
system and the worse outcome. Treat "one idea per note" as untested design convention.

## Modern personal-knowledge-management: the blunt assessment

**There is no controlled evidence that linked note systems improve idea generation,
insight, or research output**, and no HCI evaluation of bidirectional-link tools against
folders. Nobody has tested Zettelkasten against anything. Ahrens has essentially no
academic footprint. Progressive summarisation and PARA cite no empirical studies at all.
The "collector's fallacy" is a 2014 blog post — and Cajal said it better in 1897 with a
case series.

The adjacent literature is a mix of supportive and damaging, all **A**:

- **Dunlosky et al. 2013** (*PSPI* 14(1), 4–58): high utility = practice testing,
  distributed practice. **Low utility = summarisation, highlighting**, rereading. The two
  operations progressive summarisation is built from are in the low tier.
- **Nesbit & Adesope 2006** (*RER* 76(3), 413–48): 67 effect sizes, 5,818 participants —
  concept maps associated with increased retention. Their 2010 review is the sharper
  finding: constructing maps beat texts and outlines, but **studying pre-built maps showed
  no detectable effect.** The benefit is in *construction*, not consumption.
- **Whittaker & Hirschberg 2001** (*TOCHI* 8(2)): only 49 % of a paper archive was unique;
  **filers accumulated more and accessed less** than pilers — "premature filing".
- **Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014 does not replicate.** Urry et al. 2021 (*Psych Science*
  32(3)), 73 co-authors, direct replication plus mini meta-analyses over eight studies:
  the results "do not support the idea that longhand note taking improves immediate
  learning via better encoding."
- **Roscoe & Chi 2007** (*RER* 77(4), 534–74) is the finding to design around:
  tutor-learning gains are "often underwhelming" because *"peer tutors tend to exhibit a
  pervasive knowledge-telling bias… even when trained, focus more on delivering knowledge
  rather than developing it."*
- **Rozenblit & Keil 2002** (*Cognitive Science* 26(5)): the illusion of explanatory depth
  is "far stronger for explanatory knowledge than many other kinds of knowledge" — so
  forcing a *mechanistic* explanation detects exactly the class of gap a research system
  needs to detect.
- **Larkin & Simon 1987; Zhang & Norman 1994; Kirsh 1995**: externalisation changes which
  inferences are cheap. This is the strongest theoretical warrant the whole area has.

**Verdict:** the premise is well supported; the apparatus built on it is unvalidated
craft, and two of its core operations sit in the low-utility tier.

## Cross-cutting answers

**The atomic unit is whatever can be addressed.** Only addressable things can be linked,
contradicted, or reused out of context. Darwin's portfolio sheet and Leonardo's page were
too coarse and both authors lost material. Edison's page was addressable but never
indexed. Luhmann's slip is the only unit that was small, permanently addressed, *and*
cross-referenced — and its owner said the upkeep cost more than writing books. That is the
trade. The modern formalisation worth stealing is **micropublications** (Clark, Ciccarese &
Goble, *J. Biomed. Semantics* 5:28, 2014): the unit is a *claim with an argument graph* —
Claim, Statement, Qualifier, Data, Method, Reference, Attribution — with transitive
`supports` and a `challenges` edge. Their criticism of triple and nanopublication models is
precisely our risk: *"None of these models provide a means to transitively close claim
lineages to underlying empirical evidence — because they do not represent it."*

**Surprise and dissipation are the same mechanism.** Physical adjacency plus a *sparse*
index forces traversal and produces unrequested encounters; exhaustive lookup returns only
what was asked. Luhmann's operational rule: references *"must not point at the aggregating
umbrella concept but selectively pull away the material collected under it"* — a link to a
category is not a link. But Schmidt warns that without a specific question and a time
budget you "risk getting lost in the depths of the file". And Uzzi says do not maximise
novelty: conventional core, atypical tail.

**Testing understanding: jargon ablation, then mechanism.** Feynman's real test plus
Rozenblit & Keil's finding. And Roscoe & Chi's warning that an explainer will default to
knowledge-telling unless the task structure forbids it.

**Forgetting by disconnection.** Luhmann never deleted yet forgot efficiently — a decay
function implemented in topology. Darwin and Nabokov destroyed the *container* to promote
the content. Edison and Leonardo never pruned and drowned.

**Collection became synthesis where there was a deadline-bearing artefact and a forced
reduction in size** (Darwin's ladder), or where the synthesis operation was *structurally
identical* to the collection operation (Luhmann's typescripts). It failed where collection
had no terminating condition. Note what is *not* the differentiator: quality of
addressing — Benjamin and Leonardo bracket that variable.

## Transferable mechanisms

- **M1** — the addressable unit is a *claim with a support graph*, not a triple.
- **M2** — permanent addresses; topic only at the coarsest level; never mint an identifier
  from a label or a cluster.
- **M3** — capture disconfirming evidence *on encounter*, unscheduled, with provenance; a
  `challenges` edge with the same priority and citation anchoring as `supports`.
- **M4** — **jargon ablation** as the gate on "we understand this": ban the term, demand a
  restatement, score for mechanism rather than fluency.
- **M5** — force knowledge-*building*: require a prediction, a boundary condition, or a
  reconciliation; reject explanations fully entailed by the retrieved text.
- **M6** — construct the graph as the work; do not merely render it.
- **M7** — forget by disconnection, never deletion; report the dormant fraction.
- **M10** — tune the conventional-core / atypical-tail ratio explicitly.
- **M12** — cite with an edition fingerprint plus a locus; reject anchors that cannot be
  re-resolved.
- **M13** — terminate collection with a stopping rule, then a compression ladder at
  declining size limits.
- **M14** — authentic dissent from a decorrelated second pipeline, not an assigned critic.
  Nemeth, Brown & Rogers 2001: *"authentic minority was superior to all three forms of
  'devil's advocate'."*
- **M19** — an open-question ledger with dated strike-through resolution, held separately
  from findings. Faraday's queries book, and the implementable version of the "twelve
  favourite problems".

**The two design cautions to weight most heavily:** an explain-or-critique stage will
default to fluent restatement unless the task structure forbids it (Roscoe & Chi), and **a
graph handed to a reader is not the intervention the concept-map evidence supports**
(Nesbit & Adesope) — the construction is.
